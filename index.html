A warranty is a schedule you can price

Warranty language reads like a promise and behaves like a schedule: what gets covered, for how long, who pays the postage, and whether a failure ends in a repair, a replacement, or a partial credit. Praising a guarantee costs nothing and proves nothing, so the review method at DurablePicks prices durability claims in dollars per decade instead. Turned into arithmetic, the fine print stops being a mood and becomes a line item.

A worked example with the cheapest possible product. A twelve dollar pair of socks carrying a lifetime guarantee against a four dollar department store pair replaced every year. Over twenty years the guaranteed pair costs twelve dollars and the yearly habit costs eighty. That is an 85 percent saving, and on a per-decade view six dollars against forty. The money is saved by never buying replacements; the guarantee is what makes that habit rational.

Coverage decays differently by warranty type, and the spread is widest mid-life. Take a three hundred dollar machine at year five of a ten-year term. A full replacement warranty still backs the whole purchase price. A pro-rata schedule that credits half at the midpoint credits one hundred and fifty. The gap between those two documents is half the price of the product, decided entirely by wording written before the sale.

Claim friction belongs on the same ledger. A free replacement that charges six dollars in shipping, claimed three times over a product's life, adds eighteen dollars of real cost to a twelve dollar item, one and a half times the sticker price. Reading the schedule the way a lender reads a loan catches this before purchase: term, payout, exclusions, and who pays to file.
